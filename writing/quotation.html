When you prove a claim about a text, your evidence is the text’s own words — a quotation. Your job is to weave that quotation into a sentence of your own, so it reads as one smooth thought, and then to explain it. A quote dropped on the page and left to speak for itself proves nothing.

Dropped in vs. woven in
The single most common quotation mistake is the “hit-and-run” quote — a quotation left standing on its own, doing no work. Compare:
Three ways to bring a quote in
All three are correct. Pick whichever fits the sentence you’re writing.

Signal phrase, then a comma

Introduce the quote with a phrase naming who’s “speaking,” then a comma.
Frost writes, “Nature’s first green is gold.”Signal verbs: writes, argues, describes, calls, notes, observes.

Blend a fragment into your own sentence

Quote just the few words doing the work, folded into your grammar with no comma at all.
Frost calls the new leaves “gold,” something rare and precious.This is usually the smoothest option, and it puts the most pressure on choosing exactly the right words to quote.

Full sentence, then a colon

When your own complete sentence sets the quote up, use a colon.
The poem ends on a flat verdict: “Nothing gold can stay.”

The punctuation that trips everyone

Commas and periods go inside the quotation marks

American usage, and the one people get wrong most.
“Her hardest hue to hold,” Frost writes.  → comma and period both inside the quotes.
One exception is coming: once you add an MLA in-text citation in parentheses, the period moves to after the parenthesis — “Nothing gold can stay” (Frost). We’ll cover citing later; until then, the period stays inside.

Quote small

Quote only the words that matter — often three or four of them. Short quotes are easier to weave into your own sentence and easier to analyze, and a short quote you explain does more work than a long one you don’t.

Keep the words exact

What’s inside the quotation marks must match the text precisely — spelling, wording, and all. If you change or add anything to make it fit, that’s a job for brackets and ellipses (a skill for later); for now, choose a quote that already fits.

Then explain it — always

A woven-in quote becomes proof only when you say why it supports your claim. That’s the reasoning — the sentence after the quote that connects it back to your argument. Evidence answers “what in the text?”; reasoning answers “so what?” Leave the reasoning out and you have quoted the text without arguing anything.

So every quotation takes the same three steps, in this order: introduce it, quote it, explain it.
