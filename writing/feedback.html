Iteration is the third principle of this course — polished writing gets re-written — and feedback is what drives the revision. But feedback only helps if you can act on it. Buried under a hundred marks, nobody revises anything. So here’s how it works.

How my feedback works
Tap the card for the full philosophy.
“I want you to know I read your work — that I value the time, thought, effort, and vulnerability in it. Then: one or two big things to focus on, a few margin notes, a rubric. If something’s not clear, let’s have a conference.”
Running a peer review that helps

For longer pieces we’ll do brief, focused peer sessions. Done well, a classmate can see things you can’t see yourself — but only if the session is set up right. Here’s the shape:

Come with an excerpt, not everything

Choose the specific part you want eyes on — the paragraph you’re unsure of, the opening that might not land. A whole draft and the question “thoughts?” gets you a shrug.

Ask for exactly the feedback you want

As the writer, you drive. “Can you tell what I’m arguing here?” “Does the ending feel earned?” A specific question gets a specific, useful answer. Treat your classmate’s reaction as one honest reader’s experience, which is exactly what it is.

As the reader, report your experience

Be the reader you’d want. Root what you say in the assignment and in what we’ve discussed, rather than in personal taste. Describe what happened to you as you read, and be as specific about what worked as about what didn’t: “I got a little lost right here, because…”

Be generous, but honest

If you noticed something that could help a classmate, tell them; keeping it to yourself does them a disservice. You can be kind and honest at the same time. Say the true thing, gently.

And when writing about it helps

After a draft or a peer session, a little metacognitive free writing — what changed in my thinking? what am I going to try next? — turns feedback into a plan. That’s the loop closing: notice, draft, respond, revise, and around again.
