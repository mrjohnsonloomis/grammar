These are the everyday shapes — the ones worth being able to reach for without thinking about it. None of them is complicated. What matters is knowing which question each one forces you to answer, because that question is the actual work.

A list asks what else? A cluster asks what goes with what? A grid asks compared on what? A diagram asks what does what to what? A drawing asks what does it actually look like? A mandala asks what’s at the center? Pick the graphic whose question you can’t yet answer.

The six shapes
Sketches, not templates — yours will be messier, and should be.

Lists

Capturing information without structure. One thing per line, no order, no hierarchy — you don’t have to know how the ideas relate before you’re allowed to write them down.

When there’s more in your head than you can hold

Clusters

Finding connections and common ground. Loop the notes that belong together, then loop the loops. Naming a cluster is the hard part — and a good name is usually a claim in disguise.

When a list has gotten long

Grids

To build comparisons. Things down the side, questions across the top. The real payoff is the empty box — the comparison you hadn’t thought to make until the page asked for it.

When you’re holding two things side by side

Diagrams

Exploring links and connections, or working out how something functions. Boxes for parts, arrows for relationships — and the arrow is the content, so label it.

When you care about cause, order, or a loop

Drawings & Illustrations

Bring life to your page, or translate what’s in your mind’s eye. Getting the picture out where you can look at it shows you the parts you couldn’t picture — often the interesting ones.

When you’re picturing something as you read

Mandalas

To think about how all the parts fit together in a unified whole. One thing at the center, everything else placed near or far by how it relates to that center.

When you have the parts but not the whole

Choosing one
Not a rule, a shortcut. Read down the left column until one of these sounds like the trouble you’re in.

“I have too much in my head to start.”

A list. Stop organizing and start emptying — generating and sorting are different jobs, and doing them at once is what stalls you.

“I have a page of notes and no idea what they add up to.”

A cluster. Circle what belongs together, then name each circle. The names are where your ideas are hiding.

“These two things are similar but I can’t say how.”

A grid. Force the comparison into rows and columns, and pay attention to the boxes you can’t fill.

“I know the parts but not how they act on each other.”

A diagram. Draw the arrows and label every one. An unlabeled arrow is a relationship you haven’t worked out yet.

“I keep picturing this and my words keep flattening it.”

A drawing. Sketch it badly and fast, then write about the part you couldn’t draw.

“I have everything and no idea what matters most.”

A mandala. Put one thing in the center and arrange the rest around it. Then do it again with a different center.

Try it this week

In your notebook — this page can’t draw for you, and that’s rather the point. Three that fit work we’re already doing:

  • Turn an inventory into a cluster. Take a page of Overview & Inventory noticings, circle the ones that belong together, and name each circle in four words or fewer. One of those names is probably your topic sentence.
  • Grid two drafts. Your draft down one side, a classmate’s down the other; across the top, three questions you both had to answer. Fill it in and look at the differences, not the scores.
  • Mandala a whole text. Put the title in the center and place every detail you remember at the distance it deserves. What ended up on the outer ring, and are you sure it belongs there?
