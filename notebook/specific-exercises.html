The general graphics are shapes you reach for. These are procedures you run — each one has a starting move, and each one reliably gets you somewhere you weren’t. Use them when the page is blank and staying blank, or when you’ve been circling the same thought for ten minutes.

The 4WH Tool
A traditional journalist’s tool for making sure a story has no holes — borrowed here to explore a subject, an idea, or a question.

The shape

Five words spaced out on one page — corners and center works well. Leave real room under each. The empty space is doing a job: it tells you how much you haven’t said.

Five minutes, one page, no erasing

How to run it

1. Name the subject at the top — a character, a moment you might write about, a question you’re stuck on.
2. Answer each of the five in whatever order they come. Don’t force the order; the order they arrive in is information.
3. Go back and look at which one you answered last, or shortest, or not at all.

That thin spot is almost always where the writing wants to go. If you can name who and what in a sentence each but can’t manage how at all, you’ve just found out that you don’t yet understand the mechanism — which is a much more useful thing to know than a full page of easy answers.

On the missing question
You may have seen this tool with a sixth question, Why, attached. We leave it off on purpose here. Why tends to arrive as an answer you already had, and it will crowd out the other five if you let it in early. Run the four W’s and the H first; ask why once the page is full.
Mind Mapping & Concept Mapping
Two things that look similar on the page and do genuinely different work. The difference is the labels on the lines.

Mind map — for generating

One idea in the middle. Main branches off the center, smaller twigs off those. You follow a branch as far as it will go, then come back to the middle and start another.

Everything is defined by its distance from the center, and nothing has to connect sideways. That’s the freedom of it — and the limit.

Use it when you need more ideas

Concept map — for understanding

Nodes anywhere on the page, any node free to connect to any other — but every line gets a label saying how the two relate.

Those labels are the whole exercise. Writing “leads to” instead of “relates to” forces you to commit to a claim, and you’ll feel the difference immediately.

Use it when you have the ideas and need the relationships

You’ve already read a concept map on this site: the map of how the sentence moves stack. Notice how much of its meaning sits in the lines and their arrangement rather than in the boxes — that’s the form doing its job.

If you’re stuck between them
Start with a mind map, then convert it. Redraw the same material as a concept map and force yourself to label every line. Half your branches will turn out to have been “and also,” which is worth discovering.
Creating Visual Metaphors
Borrow the shape of an object you can picture, and use it to hold an idea you can’t.

The snowglobe

A small sealed world you look into from the outside. Shake it and everything swirls, then settles more or less where it was.

Good for a memoir moment or the world of a text. What’s inside the glass? What’s outside it? Who is holding it? And what counts as the shake — the event that stirs everything and leaves the world, maybe, unchanged?

For a contained moment or world

The hamburger

You’ve met this one — a top, a filling, a bottom. It’s a metaphor about layers and order, and it has been handed to you as a picture of a paragraph since about fourth grade.

Which makes it the perfect one to test. A hamburger says your first and last sentences are basically the same bread. Is that true of a paragraph you’re proud of?

For structure — and for arguing with
Find the leak

Every metaphor holds in some places and leaks in others, and the leak is where you learn something. A metaphor you accept teaches you nothing; a metaphor you push until it breaks tells you what the real thing does that the picture can’t.

So the exercise has three moves, and only the third one is hard:

Draw it

Sketch the object — snowglobe, hamburger, iceberg, tide, whatever you’ve chosen — and label the parts with the parts of your idea. Ten minutes, no more.

Push it

Ask the questions the object invites but your idea didn’t: who’s holding the snowglobe? what happens to a hamburger with no filling? The picture will generate questions your outline never would.

Say where it breaks

Write one sentence: “This metaphor stops working when ______.” That sentence is usually the most honest thing on the page, and it’s frequently the beginning of a real claim.

The three exercises, in detail

Try it this week
  • 4WH a character you can’t get a handle on. Then write for five minutes about the question you couldn’t answer.
  • Convert a mind map to a concept map. Same material, but every line gets a label. Note which connections you couldn’t name.
  • Invent your own metaphor for something we’re reading — not snowglobe, not hamburger. Draw it, label it, and finish the sentence “this stops working when…”.

These exercises happen on paper. Nothing on this site is saved or sent anywhere — and none of this work should be happening on a screen in the first place. It belongs in the notebook, where you can see the whole page at once. Framing and source notes are on N2 · Visual Notes.
