You use the notebook to find out what you think. That is different work from recording what you already know, and it runs on different rules. Nothing in here is graded. Most of it nobody reads but you. You will fill pages you never use again, and that is the practice working as intended.

“Keep the pen moving. Even if you’re stuck, write about being stuck.”
Two ways of working on the page

In words

Free writing: private, focused, and metacognitive. Sentences do most of the work most of the time, and the discipline of keeping the pen moving is what makes them arrive.

In shapes

Visual notes: lists, clusters, grids, diagrams, drawings, maps. Some thoughts contradict each other, or stack, or are still hazy — a page of prose forces them into a single line, and a graphic doesn’t have to.

You don’t have to choose between them when you open a page. Most good notebook pages are both: a list that grows a paragraph off the side of it, or a paragraph that stalls and turns into a diagram. Use whichever holds the thought.

The rules of the notebook

One notebook, all term

A single notebook that is just for English, brought every day. The record only works if it’s all in one place — and carrying it is part of treating writing as a daily practice.

You choose what to share

You’ll sometimes share notebook writing, but never all of it and never without warning. Bracket the part you’re willing to read out — a paragraph, a sentence, a phrase — and the rest stays yours.

Nothing here is graded as a product

Handwriting, spelling, and drawing quality don’t count for anything. A notebook page is a tool you used.

The modules
The notebook runs all year, alongside the sentence lessons and the writing modules — it’s where most of the raw material for both of them comes from.
