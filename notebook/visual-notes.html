I recently came across a book about having meetings facilitated by people who doodle and draw while participants are sharing ideas. (Appropriately, it’s called Visual Meetings, by David Sibbet.) While we’re not always having “meetings” in our class, some elements of his ideas resonate with our approach to notebooks more broadly. Namely:

Working visually is deeply integrative — it combines both visual (right brain) and verbal (left brain) ways of operating with interaction and physical movement.

Graphic displays can contain contradictory information on the same sheet of paper, softening the either/or thinking that our spoken language reinforces.

Working with graphic metaphors allows people to talk directly about how they are making sense of things.

David Sibbet, Visual Meetings
One small edit
In that last bullet, we could easily replace “talk” with “think” or “write.” That’s the whole idea of this page: the graphic isn’t there to explain something you already worked out. It’s there so you can work it out.
A language that’s still being invented

Sibbet quotes the writer Bob Horn, from his book Visual Language:

Visual language is emerging as any other language does — by people creating it and speaking it… it is being born of people’s need, worldwide, to deal with complex ideas that are difficult to express in text alone.

Robert E. Horn, Visual Language, quoted in Sibbet

You will have ideas that are difficult to express in text alone — or a series of thoughts and ideas that contradict each other, or build on top of one another, or add haziness to your thinking. In these moments, it might help to have visual cues in your notebook to facilitate your thinking.

Three things this is not

Not decoration

You’re not illustrating a finished thought so it looks nicer. If the drawing comes after the thinking, it’s a diagram of your notes. We want the drawing to be the thinking.

Not an art assignment

Nobody is grading the drawing, and “I can’t draw” doesn’t disqualify you. Boxes, arrows, circles, and stick figures do almost all of the work here.

Not a replacement for writing

Sentences are still where most of your thinking will live. A graphic is what you reach for when the sentence keeps coming out wrong — usually because the idea isn’t linear yet.
